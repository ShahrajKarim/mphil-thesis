\section{Related Literature}

This thesis relates to three strands of literature: public procurement and pharmaceutical pricing, the relationship between market size and innovation, and the dynamic determinants of firm innovation incentives.

\subsection{Public Procurement and Pharmaceutical Pricing}

This thesis first relates to the literature on public insurance and pharmaceutical procurement. A large body of work studies how public payers shape drug prices, utilisation, and firm behaviour through reimbursement rules, formulary design, and bargaining institutions. In the Medicaid setting, this is especially important because statutory and supplemental rebates mean that public coverage expansion affects not only demand, but also the prices manufacturers ultimately receive.

The institutional foundation of this thesis builds on early work demonstrating how Medicaid's pricing rules distort the broader pharmaceutical market. For instance, \cite{duggan2006} show that the Medicaid "best price" rules fundamentally alter firms' pricing strategies, while \cite{ellison2010} highlight the role of countervailing buyer power in allowing large purchasers to extract steep discounts from pharmaceutical suppliers. More recently, empirical work has evaluated how states aggressively utilise Preferred Drug Lists (PDLs) and multi-state purchasing pools to maximise their monopsony power and extract supplemental rebates in exchange for market access.

This literature establishes that Medicaid and related public programmes do not simply expand insurance coverage; they also fundamentally alter the terms on which drugs are purchased. However, much of this work focuses on the static consequences of public bargaining power for prices and state expenditures. By contrast, this thesis examines how these specific procurement institutions may also affect firms' intertemporal innovation incentives.

\subsection{Market Size and Pharmaceutical Innovation}

The thesis also relates to the foundational literature linking market size to innovation. A standard result in the economics of innovation is that larger markets increase the expected return to successful R\&D, thereby stimulating investment. This principle of directed technical change \citep{acemoglu2002} has been robustly supported by empirical work in the health economics literature. \cite{finkelstein2004} demonstrates that policies increasing the potential market for vaccines spurred a significant increase in new clinical trials, while \cite{dubois2015} show that larger potential market sizes lead to a proportionally higher number of new pharmaceutical entities being introduced. 

This thesis builds on that literature but studies an important institutional qualification. In the Medicaid setting, market expansion does not operate solely through a larger sales base. It also strengthens the bargaining position of the public buyer. As the state leverages its expanded market share to extract deeper supplemental rebates, the monopoly rents associated with successful innovation may be compressed. 

The thesis therefore studies a setting in which the usual market-size effect—the "pull" incentive for innovation—may be weakened, offset, or even reversed by procurement-induced reductions in post-approval profits. In a public procurement environment, the link between market size and innovation depends critically on how much of the additional surplus is captured by the buyer.

\subsection{Dynamic Innovation Incentives}

Finally, the thesis relates to work on the dynamic determinants of innovation incentives. A broad literature argues that a firm's R\&D investment depends not only on current market conditions, but also on its financial position, its competitive state, and the continuation value generated by past success.

A key friction in pharmaceutical R\&D is the cost of capital. \cite{hal2002financing} argues that R\&D is particularly sensitive to financing frictions because it is risky, intangible, and opaque to outside investors; therefore, internal cash flow materially affects innovative capability. Furthermore, models of dynamic industry competition \citep{ericson1995markov} and step-by-step innovation \citep{budd1993model, aghion2001competition} demonstrate that a firm's current competitive position heavily dictates its future investment incentives. 

The dynamic extension of the theoretical model draws on these ideas by treating procurement outcomes as state variables for future innovation. In particular, securing PDL placement affects future R\&D by providing the internal cash flows required to bypass capital market frictions, while also building accumulated organisational and regulatory capability. In this sense, the thesis connects the literature on dynamic innovation incentives directly to the institutional setting of public pharmaceutical procurement. The empirical finding that firms securing PDL placement innovate more in subsequent periods is highly consistent with this state-dependent mechanism, demonstrating how contemporary procurement outcomes shape the future distribution of innovative effort across firms.
